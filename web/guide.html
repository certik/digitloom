The mnemonic major system

A number you can picture.

A string of digits is abstract. An image is something your memory can work with. The major system connects ten digits to ten families of speech sounds, so you can turn a number into words and imagine a scene.

Start with a small scene

3277 becomes moon cake. In moon, m gives 3 and n gives 2. In cake, the two k sounds give 77.

Picture a cake so enormous that it rests on the moon. The stranger the scene feels to you, the easier it may be to bring back later.

Build your own memory thread

Hear the word, then read the digits

Pronunciation decides the code. For example, the final sound of ice is s, so its code is 0. Silent letters do not add digits. Most vowels, along with h, w, and y sounds, can be ignored.

Count repeated sounds separately. The s and z in says give 00, even with a vowel between them. DigitLoom uses CMUdict's mostly North American pronunciations; a word can have more than one valid code when the dictionary gives different ways to say it.

Choose how much to remember at once

Enter your digits in the word builder to see suggestions immediately. The input moves up to make room for words, and your memory thread and progress appear once you make a choice. Open Sound key beside the input label for a quick reminder of the sound families.

Suggestions cover the beginning of the unencoded part, grouped by code length. The longest groups come first; the more common words lead each group. Words with a wordfreq Zipf score of at least 3.5 have a green background by default. This is roughly three occurrences per million words. There is no fixed number of highlights: a group can have many, one, or none. Lower-frequency and unranked words remain available. Frequency is a guide, not a guarantee of personal familiarity or vivid imagery. A group's heading tells you how many digits its words cover. Each button's description gives the exact code.

Choose a word and the remaining digits get their own suggestions. The builder brings the next choices into view. Undo word takes back one choice. Clear starts a new thread. Editing the number also starts over. Spaces are ignored during input; leading zeros remain part of the number.

Words cover at least two digits while two or more remain. When just one digit remains, one-digit words are offered. There is no five-digit limit: long words can cover longer stretches, and a sequence can encode a number of any length.

Change one word, keep the rest

Click a selected word to replace it without rebuilding the sentence. Its starting digit stays fixed, with same-length choices shown first. Open Other lengths for shorter or longer alternatives. Other words never move automatically.

A shorter replacement can leave a dashed Unassigned block. Click it to fill those digits; even a one-digit gap in the middle can have its own word. A longer replacement can share digits with a neighbor. Both words show Overlap, and the coverage strip identifies the shared positions. The input never duplicates those digits. A thread is complete only when every digit is covered exactly once.

Click a neighboring word and choose Fit between neighbors to preview the available span after preceding words and before the next word. Nothing changes until you choose a replacement. Use current position restores the original replacement span; Cancel or Escape leaves the selection untouched. If there is no space between neighbors, remove or adjust a word first.

Suppose 4350 29 345 23 405 97 is harmless / unhappy / merely / naomi / russel / buck. Replace unhappy (29) with niobium (293). It overlaps the first digit of merely (345), but all the other words remain in place.

Click merely, choose Fit between neighbors: 45, and select real (45). Now the number splits as 4350 293 45 23 405 97. The changed middle fits again, without redoing harmless, naomi, russel, or buck. Turn off Common words only to see niobium, which is below the default cutoff.

Remove word removes just the word being edited. Undo change reverses the latest replacement or removal; Undo word reverses an addition. Filters still apply to replacement choices. Auto split is only for adding words at the end, so editing also offers one-digit words and other lengths while keeping the rest of the thread intact.

Show just the common words

Open Filter beside the input and check Common words only to hide the white alternatives and any empty length groups. Word order does not change, and every word meeting the cutoff remains available without a fixed quota.

Use Frequency cutoff to adjust the Zipf threshold from 1.0 to 8.0 in 0.1 steps. Lower it for more green choices, or raise it for fewer. Reset 3.5 returns to the default. Words with no frequency data stay unhighlighted and are hidden in common-only mode.

Changing these controls keeps the words you have already selected. If no choices meet the cutoff, lower it or choose Show all words. Clear keeps your filter settings for the next number. These settings are not saved in browser storage or added to the page address.

Split a long number automatically

Turn on Auto split beside the input label to suggest mixed two-, three-, and four-digit word chunks. The builder adds spaces at their boundaries and shows words for the next chunk only. You still choose each word; it does not invent a sentence for you.

For example, 952147132 splits into 9521 471 32, which can become planet rocket moon. The split prefers longer available chunks, but checks the rest of the number too. A one-digit ending is used only when no complete split avoids it. This chooses boundaries, not the most memorable phrase.

With Common words only enabled, Auto split looks for a complete split using words at your chosen cutoff. For example, at 3.5 it splits 3277 into 32 77 rather than choosing a rare four-digit word. If no complete common-word split exists, lower the cutoff or show all words.

Switch Auto split off to explore all word lengths again. Switching modes keeps your selected words and changes only the remaining digits. Undo word restores the previous digits and replans them; editing the number starts over. Clear keeps your chosen mode. If no complete automatic split exists, the builder asks you to turn Auto split off rather than silently changing modes.

A little help from grammar

A small text label beneath a word lists its possible roles, such as "noun", "verb", or "noun, verb, adjective". All word spellings use the same typography; the labels carry the grammatical information. An unlabeled word has no matching hint. These are possibilities from WordNet, not a grammar check or a promise that a particular phrase makes sense.

Scenes with person, action, object

The PAO page uses a different technique for numbers you want to recall quickly. The word builder looks up words whose sounds match the digits, so you can encode any number without preparation. PAO instead relies on a table you prepare and learn ahead of time. Its 110 rows give every single digit from 0 to 9 and every pair from 00 to 99 three associations: a person, an action, and an object. A number is read in pairs, with one final single digit if its length is odd. Each code continues the person, action, object cycle. Three codes make one scene, and the next code starts another.

327753 becomes an astronaut who frosts a lime. In DigitLoom's starter table, 32 has the person astronaut, 77 the action frosts, and 53 the object lime.

Each code also has a peg, a major-system word for the pair: moon, cake, and lime here. A peg helps you remember why an association belongs to its code; scenes use the associations.

Try the starter table

Associations are personal and should stay fixed once learned. They do not need to share the sounds of their code. A table might give 88 the person musician, 53 the action squeezes, and 66 the object defendant, so 885366 becomes a musician who squeezes a defendant. Rehearse your table until each pair brings back all three associations at once; after that, six digits fit in a single image.

Roles describe positions in the scene, not grammar. A person can be an animal or a cartoon character, such as Wile E. Coyote for 08, and an object can be a person. DigitLoom does not guess roles from the noun and verb labels in its dictionary, and PAO associations never become part of the word library.

How the PAO page reads a number

Type digits and the scenes appear at once. Each scene card lists its person, action, and object with their exact codes. Leading zeros are kept, spaces are ignored, and nothing is padded. If the number ends partway through a scene, the last scene has just a person, or a person and an action. A final single digit uses its own PAO row in the next role, without resetting the cycle or adding a zero. For example, 324 uses the action for 4, flails; 32774 uses its object, his light saber; and 3277534 starts a new scene with its person, Luke Skywalks. 0 and 00 are different codes. No separate dictionary choice is needed.

Blank associations are listed as missing. A value used for two codes in the same role is flagged as ambiguous, because the scene could then decode two ways. Each warning leads to the table cell that needs attention. Progress counts two digits for a resolved pair and one for a resolved single-digit code, so a number is not called encoded until every part is settled. Long numbers are encoded in full and shown fifty scenes at a time.

Make the table your own

Choose Edit table to rename the table or change any peg, person, action, or object. Find and Show narrow the 110 codes to a word, single digits, pairs, codes in your number, missing associations, or duplicates. Ideas beside a peg opens the dictionary and lists the words whose sounds encode exactly that code, with green backgrounds for common words. A change applies to every number you encode with the table.

Your table lives only in the open tab. DigitLoom does not save it in your browser or send it anywhere, so use Export table to download digitloom-pao.json and Import table to continue later. The file holds the table with its license and attribution, never the numbers you encoded. New blank table starts from scratch and Restore starter brings back DigitLoom's example. Before replacing changes you have not exported, DigitLoom asks first.

Older 100-row table files still import. Their pair associations and attribution stay intact; the new single-digit rows start blank for you to fill. The current starter uses contributed single-digit associations. To avoid collisions, its generated person for 14 is now woodpecker, its action for 37 is brews, and its action for 99 is gnaws. Imported tables are never changed to match those starter choices.

Local by design

DigitLoom downloads its dictionary once and does the matching in your browser. Your numbers and selected words are not sent to a service or saved in an account. PAO tables stay in the open tab unless you export them. The dictionary includes all usable pronunciations in our pinned CMUdict source, not every English word.
