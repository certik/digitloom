The mnemonic major system

A number you can picture.

A string of digits is abstract. An image is something your memory can work with. The major system connects ten digits to ten families of speech sounds, so you can turn a number into words and imagine a scene.

Start with a small scene

3277 becomes moon cake. In moon, m gives 3 and n gives 2. In cake, the two k sounds give 77.

Picture a cake so enormous that it rests on the moon. The stranger the scene feels to you, the easier it may be to bring back later.

Build your own memory thread

Hear the word, then read the digits

Pronunciation decides the code. For example, the final sound of ice is s, so its code is 0. Silent letters do not add digits. Most vowels, along with h, w, and y sounds, can be ignored.

Count repeated sounds separately. The s and z in says give 00, even with a vowel between them. DigitLoom uses CMUdict's mostly North American pronunciations; a word can have more than one valid code when the dictionary gives different ways to say it.

Choose how much to remember at once

Enter your digits in the word builder to see suggestions immediately. The input moves up to make room for words, and your memory thread and progress appear once you make a choice. Open Sound key beside the input label for a quick reminder of the sound families.

Suggestions cover the beginning of the unencoded part, grouped by code length. The longest groups come first; the more common words lead each group. Words with a wordfreq Zipf score of at least 3.5 have a green background. This is roughly three occurrences per million words. There is no fixed number of highlights: a group can have many, one, or none. Lower-frequency and unranked words remain available. Frequency is a guide, not a guarantee of personal familiarity or vivid imagery. A group's heading tells you how many digits its words cover. Each button's description gives the exact code.

Choose a word and the remaining digits get their own suggestions. The builder returns to the top so the next choices are in view. Undo word takes back one choice. Clear starts a new thread. Editing the number also starts over. Spaces are ignored during input; leading zeros remain part of the number.

Words cover at least two digits while two or more remain. When just one digit remains, one-digit words are offered. There is no five-digit limit: long words can cover longer stretches, and a sequence can encode a number of any length.

Split a long number automatically

Turn on Auto split beside the input label to suggest mixed two-, three-, and four-digit word chunks. The builder adds spaces at their boundaries and shows words for the next chunk only. You still choose each word; it does not invent a sentence for you.

For example, 952147132 splits into 9521 471 32, which can become planet rocket moon. The split prefers longer available chunks, but checks the rest of the number too. A one-digit ending is used only when no complete split avoids it. This chooses boundaries, not the most memorable phrase.

Switch Auto split off to explore all word lengths again. Switching modes keeps your selected words and changes only the remaining digits. Undo word restores the previous digits and replans them; editing starts over. Clear keeps your chosen mode. If no complete automatic split exists, the builder asks you to turn Auto split off rather than silently changing modes.

A little help from grammar

A small text label beneath a word lists its possible roles, such as "noun", "verb", or "noun, verb, adjective". All word spellings use the same typography; the labels carry the grammatical information. An unlabeled word has no matching hint. These are possibilities from WordNet, not a grammar check or a promise that a particular phrase makes sense.

Scenes with person, action, object

The PAO page uses a different technique for numbers you want to recall quickly. The word builder looks up words whose sounds match the digits, so you can encode any number without preparation. PAO instead relies on a table you prepare and learn ahead of time. It gives every pair from 00 to 99 three associations: a person, an action, and an object. A number is then read two digits at a time. The first pair supplies a person, the second an action, and the third an object. Together they make one scene, and the next pair starts another.

327753 becomes an astronaut who frosts a lime. In DigitLoom's starter table, 32 has the person astronaut, 77 the action frosts, and 53 the object lime.

Each code also has a peg, a major-system word for the pair: moon, cake, and lime here. A peg helps you remember why an association belongs to its code; scenes use the associations.

Try the starter table

Associations are personal and should stay fixed once learned. They do not need to share the sounds of their pair. A table might give 88 the person musician, 53 the action squeezes, and 66 the object defendant, so 885366 becomes a musician who squeezes a defendant. Rehearse your table until each pair brings back all three associations at once; after that, six digits fit in a single image.

Roles describe positions in the scene, not grammar. A person can be an animal or a cartoon character, such as Wile E. Coyote for 08, and an object can be a person. DigitLoom does not guess roles from the noun and verb labels in its dictionary, and PAO associations never become part of the word library.

How the PAO page reads a number

Type digits and the scenes appear at once. Each scene card lists its person, action, and object with their exact pairs. Leading zeros are kept, spaces are ignored, and nothing is padded. If the number ends partway through a scene, the last scene has just a person, or a person and an action. One leftover digit has no pair, so DigitLoom offers ordinary one-digit words for it, as the word builder does. This ending is separate from your scenes; you can choose it, undo it, or edit the number to start again.

Blank associations are listed as missing. A value used for two codes in the same role is flagged as ambiguous, because the scene could then decode two ways. Each warning leads to the table cell that needs attention. Progress counts only resolved pairs and a chosen ending, so a number is not called encoded until every part is settled. Long numbers are encoded in full and shown fifty scenes at a time.

Make the table your own

Choose Edit table to rename the table or change any peg, person, action, or object. Find and Show narrow the hundred codes to a word, the pairs in your number, missing associations, or duplicates. Ideas beside a peg opens the dictionary and lists the words whose sounds encode exactly that pair, with green backgrounds for common words. A change applies to every number you encode with the table.

Your table lives only in the open tab. DigitLoom does not save it in your browser or send it anywhere, so use Export table to download digitloom-pao.json and Import table to continue later. The file holds the table with its license and attribution, never the numbers you encoded. New blank table starts from scratch and Restore starter brings back DigitLoom's example. Before replacing changes you have not exported, DigitLoom asks first.

Local by design

DigitLoom downloads its dictionary once and does the matching in your browser. Your numbers and selected words are not sent to a service or saved in an account. PAO tables stay in the open tab unless you export them. The dictionary includes all usable pronunciations in our pinned CMUdict source, not every English word.
